Central Limit Theorem and Its Implications for SGD

The CLT explains why the Gaussian appears everywhere and provides theoretical grounding for confidence intervals, SGD noise structure, and learning rate scaling rules.

Statement of the Central Limit Theorem

For iid X₁,…,Xₙ with mean μ and variance σ² < ∞:

Zₙ = √n·(X̄ₙ−μ)/σ →_d N(0,1) as n→∞

X̄ₙ is approximately N(μ, σ²/n) for large n, regardless of the distribution of X.

Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN)

X̄ₙ →_p μ: P(|X̄ₙ−μ| > ε) → 0

Chebyshev proof: P(|X̄ₙ−μ| ≥ ε) ≤ Var(X̄ₙ)/ε² = σ²/(nε²) → 0

Rate O(1/n) for probability, requires only finite variance.

Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN)

X̄ₙ → μ almost surely: P(lim_{n→∞} X̄ₙ = μ) = 1

Stronger than WLLN — every sample path converges, not just in distribution. Requires only E[|X|] < ∞.

Convergence modes (weakest to strongest): in distribution → in probability → a.s. → L².

Berry-Esseen Theorem — Rate of Convergence

sup_z |P(Zₙ ≤ z) − Φ(z)| ≤ C·ρ/(σ³√n)

where ρ = E[|X−μ|³], C ≤ 0.4748.

Skewed distributions (p=0.01 Bernoulli, log-normal) need n >> 30 for the Gaussian approximation. Always verify with a QQ-plot or KS test.

CLT Justification for Gaussian Noise

Additive noise = sum of many small independent perturbations → CLT → Gaussian. This justifies the Gaussian noise assumption in linear regression. Breaks down for heavy-tailed errors (financial returns, earthquake magnitudes).

CLT for Stochastic Gradient Descent

Minibatch gradient estimate: ĝ = (1/B)Σ_{i∈B} ∇ℓ(θ;xᵢ) ≈ N(g, Σ_g/B) for large B.

  1. Large B → small, nearly Gaussian, isotropic noise
  2. Small B → larger, non-Gaussian, anisotropic noise
  3. Gradient noise biases toward flat minima (implicit regularisation)
  4. Batch-LR scaling: doubling B → scale LR by √2 (noise-scale rule) or 2 (linear scaling rule)
# SGD gradient noise vs batch size
true_grad, grad_std = 1.0, 5.0
for B in [1, 4, 16, 64, 256]:
    grad_estimates = [np.mean(np.random.normal(true_grad, grad_std, B)) for _ in range(10000)]
    print(f"B={B:4d}: Std[g]={np.std(grad_estimates):.3f} (theory: {grad_std/np.sqrt(B):.3f})")

CLT and ERM Generalisation

ERM training loss → true expected risk by SLLN. CLT gives the standard error of estimated accuracy:

SE(accuracy) = √(p(1−p)/nₜₑₛₜ)

For p=0.9, nₜₑₛₜ=1000: SE≈0.0095. Differences < 2·SE are not statistically significant.

PAC learning bounds use concentration inequalities (Hoeffding, McDiarmid) for non-asymptotic, bounded-loss guarantees.
