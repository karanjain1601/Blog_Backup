LLM Output Watermarking

LLM watermarking embeds a statistical signal into generated text that is imperceptible to human readers but detectable by an algorithm with access to a secret key. Unlike metadata-based provenance (embedding model version in an API header), text watermarking is intrinsic to the content — it survives copy-paste, screenshot-to-text, and reposting across platforms. The central challenge is the tension between three desiderata that are largely in conflict: detection power (the signal must be strong enough to detect reliably), text quality (the signal must not degrade fluency, coherence, or factual accuracy), and robustness (the signal must survive reasonable edits such as synonym substitution, paraphrasing, or sentence reordering). No current method achieves all three simultaneously, and the choice of watermarking scheme is a deliberate tradeoff among them.

Overview

Watermarking LLM outputs has become an active area following the widespread deployment of language models capable of generating text indistinguishable from human writing at scale. The threat addressed is not malicious use per se, but attribution: given a text, can one determine whether it was generated by a specific model? Applications include academic integrity (detecting AI-written essays), disinformation attribution (tracing AI-generated propaganda), content licensing (verifying that generated content complies with terms of service), and regulatory compliance (jurisdictions requiring disclosure of AI-generated content). The NIST AI Risk Management Framework and proposed EU AI Act both include provisions around AI content provenance, making watermarking a potential compliance requirement for large-scale LLM deployments.

Watermarking schemes for text differ fundamentally from image watermarking because text is discrete — small perturbations that are imperceptible in images (tweaking a pixel value) do not exist for text. Any modification to a token changes the word. This means text watermarks must operate at the level of token selection during generation: bias the sampling process toward a subset of tokens that carry a detectable signal, without making the resulting text obviously unnatural. The two broad families are generation-time watermarks (altering the sampling process) and post-hoc watermarks (modifying finished text to embed a signal through synonym substitution or paraphrasing), with generation-time methods being far more powerful and reliable.

Why Watermark LLM Output

The case for watermarking rests on the difficulty of statistical detection without a watermark. Current AI-text detectors (GPTZero, Originality.ai, Turnitin AI) operate by measuring statistical properties of text — perplexity, burstiness, n-gram distributions — that correlate with AI generation but produce unacceptably high false positive rates on human text written in formal or technical register. Studies have shown false positive rates of 10-25% on non-native English speakers' academic writing and up to 50% on legal and medical documents, creating serious equity and liability concerns. Watermarking sidesteps the statistical detection problem by embedding a private signal that only the model provider (with the secret key) can detect, achieving near-zero false positives on human text because human writers do not produce the specific statistical pattern dictated by the secret key.

  • Academic integrity: detect AI-generated submissions with high precision and near-zero false positive rate on human text.
  • Disinformation tracing: attribute AI-generated propaganda, fake reviews, or synthetic media to a specific model provider.
  • Content licensing: verify that generated outputs comply with terms of service restricting AI content in specific contexts.
  • Model theft detection: confirm that a distilled or fine-tuned model used the watermarked model's outputs as training data.
  • Regulatory compliance: satisfy emerging disclosure requirements in EU AI Act, NIST RMF, and similar frameworks.
  • Copyright attribution: provide evidence that a text was AI-generated and thus ineligible for copyright protection under current law.

Green-Red Token List Watermark

The Kirchenbauer et al. (2023) green-red list watermark is the foundational algorithm in the field. At each token generation step, a hash of the preceding token (or a context window of preceding tokens) is used as a seed to deterministically partition the vocabulary into two sets of equal size: a 'green' list (50% of tokens) and a 'red' list (50% of tokens). The model's logits are then modified by adding a fixed bias δ (typically δ=2.0) to the logits of all green-list tokens before applying softmax and sampling. This makes green tokens significantly more likely to be sampled — roughly e^δ ≈ 7× more likely before normalization — while maintaining a distribution over the full vocabulary. Over a sequence of n tokens, approximately (0.5 + ε) × n tokens will be green, where ε depends on δ and the model's confidence. This green-token excess is the detectable signal.

import torch
import hashlib
import numpy as np
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

def vocab_greenlist(vocab_size, prev_token, gamma=0.5):
    '''Deterministically assign each vocab token to green (1) or red (0) via hash.'''
    seed = int(hashlib.sha256(str(prev_token).encode()).hexdigest(), 16) % (2**32)
    rng  = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    perm = rng.permutation(vocab_size)
    mask = torch.zeros(vocab_size, dtype=torch.float32)
    mask[perm[:int(vocab_size * gamma)]] = 1.0
    return mask

def greenred_generate(model, tokenizer, prompt, max_new=200,
                      delta=2.0, gamma=0.5, device='cuda'):
    '''Green-red list watermark: add delta to green token logits at each sampling step.'''
    model.eval()
    enc     = tokenizer(prompt, return_tensors='pt').to(device)
    ids     = enc['input_ids']
    vocab_n = model.config.vocab_size
    for _ in range(max_new):
        with torch.no_grad():
            logits = model(ids).logits[0, -1, :]       # (vocab,)
        prev   = ids[0, -1].item()
        gmask  = vocab_greenlist(vocab_n, prev, gamma).to(device)
        logits = logits + delta * gmask                # bias toward green tokens
        probs  = torch.softmax(logits, dim=-1)
        nxt    = torch.multinomial(probs, 1).unsqueeze(0)
        ids    = torch.cat([ids, nxt], dim=1)
        if nxt.item() == tokenizer.eos_token_id:
            break
    return tokenizer.decode(ids[0], skip_special_tokens=True)

The quality impact of the green-red watermark depends critically on δ. At δ=0, no watermark is present and text quality is unchanged. At δ=2.0, the watermark is reliably detectable in 200+ token sequences but produces a small but measurable perplexity increase — approximately 0.5-2.0 PPL points on standard benchmarks. At δ=5.0, detection is possible in 25-50 token sequences but perplexity degradation becomes noticeable, and the text may contain unnatural word choices forced by green-token bias. The practical sweet spot of δ=2.0-3.0 provides reliable detection at n≥100 tokens with sub-1-PPL quality degradation that is imperceptible in human evaluation. An important detail: the watermark is keyless in the sense that anyone who knows the hash function and green-list construction algorithm can both generate and detect watermarks — a more secure variant uses a private hash key that only the provider knows.

Statistical Detection

Watermark detection computes a z-score that measures how many more green tokens appear in a text than would be expected by chance. Under the null hypothesis (no watermark, human-written text), each token independently lands in the green list with probability γ=0.5. For a sequence of n tokens, the green count G ~ Binomial(n, 0.5), with mean γn and standard deviation √(γ(1-γ)n) = √(0.25n) = 0.5√n. The z-score is z = (G - γn) / √(γ(1-γ)n). Under the null, z ~ N(0,1). For watermarked text with δ=2.0, z grows proportionally to √n: at n=100 tokens, z≈4-6; at n=200, z≈6-9. Detection is declared at z > z_threshold, typically z_threshold=4 corresponding to p-value ≈ 3e-5, giving near-zero false positive rate on human text. The false negative rate (missing a watermarked text) depends on δ and n: at δ=2.0 and n=100, false negative rate is typically less than 5%.

import hashlib
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm
from transformers import AutoTokenizer

def token_in_greenlist(token_id, prev_token_id, vocab_size, gamma=0.5):
    '''Return True if token_id is in the green list seeded by prev_token_id.'''
    seed = int(hashlib.sha256(str(prev_token_id).encode()).hexdigest(), 16) % (2**32)
    rng  = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    perm = rng.permutation(vocab_size)
    n_green = int(vocab_size * gamma)
    return int(token_id) in set(int(x) for x in perm[:n_green])

def detect_watermark(text, tokenizer, vocab_size, gamma=0.5, z_threshold=4.0):
    '''Compute z-score and p-value for green-token excess; flag if z > threshold.'''
    ids = tokenizer(text, return_tensors='pt')['input_ids'][0].tolist()
    if len(ids) < 2:
        return {'detected': False, 'z': 0.0, 'p': 1.0, 'green_frac': 0.0}
    green_count = sum(
        token_in_greenlist(ids[i], ids[i-1], vocab_size, gamma)
        for i in range(1, len(ids))
    )
    n = len(ids) - 1
    # Under H0: green_count ~ Binomial(n, gamma); use normal approximation
    z = (green_count - gamma * n) / np.sqrt(gamma * (1.0 - gamma) * n)
    p = float(1.0 - norm.cdf(z))
    return {
        'detected':    z > z_threshold,
        'z':           round(z, 3),
        'p_value':     round(p, 8),
        'green_frac':  round(green_count / n, 3),
        'n_tokens':    n
    }

The detector requires only the tokenizer, vocabulary size, and the hash function (and optionally the secret key for private watermarks). It does not require access to the model weights or logits — making it a lightweight, scalable screening tool. A key property is that false positive rate on human text is bounded by the normal tail probability: at z_threshold=4, FPR ≈ 3.2e-5 regardless of text domain, style, or register. This is far superior to AI-text classifiers, which exhibit domain-dependent FPR as high as 25%. The detector can be tuned by adjusting z_threshold: z=3 (p≈0.0013) maximizes recall for short texts at a slightly higher FPR; z=5 (p≈3e-7) is used when false positives carry high cost (e.g., academic misconduct proceedings).

Semantic Watermarks

Semantic watermarks (Fernandez et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024) operate in the embedding space rather than the token space, producing watermarks that are more robust to synonym substitution because semantically equivalent tokens are grouped together. Instead of partitioning the vocabulary by token ID (arbitrary), semantic watermarks partition by embedding distance: for a given context seed, the vocabulary is split into green and red tokens based on the cosine similarity of their embeddings to a seeded random direction in embedding space. Tokens that are synonyms or semantically similar tend to cluster together in embedding space, so substituting a green synonym for another green synonym preserves the watermark, while the green-red token list watermark would randomly assign synonyms to different lists.

The practical improvement from semantic watermarking over token-list watermarking is most visible under paraphrase attacks. When an adversary uses a paraphrase model to rewrite watermarked text, the green-red token list watermark loses signal rapidly — synonym substitution changes a token's green/red assignment with 50% probability, so each edit has a 50% chance of converting a green token to red. Semantic watermarking reduces this attack effectiveness because synonyms that preserve meaning also tend to preserve their semantic-space partition membership. Empirically, semantic watermarks retain detection rates of 70-85% under 30% word substitution, compared to 40-60% for token-list watermarks under the same attack. The tradeoff is slightly lower detection power per token in the unattacked case, because the semantic grouping introduces correlation between tokens that reduces the effective information per observation.

Robustness to Editing

Robustness is the most studied limitation of LLM watermarks. Any watermark that embeds a statistical signal in token choices is vulnerable to attacks that change tokens while preserving meaning. The main attack vectors are: (1) synonym substitution — replacing green tokens with semantically equivalent red tokens, which is easy to automate with a lookup table or MLM; (2) paraphrase generation — using another LLM to rewrite the watermarked text from scratch, breaking token-level statistics while preserving semantic content; (3) sentence reordering — changing the order of sentences, which changes the prev-token hash seeds used to construct green lists; (4) text completion attacks — inserting the watermarked text into a larger human-written document, diluting the green-token fraction toward 0.5. At sufficient edit rates, all current watermarks lose detection power — the fundamental limitation is that any watermark that survives heavy editing must embed its signal at a semantic level, but semantic-level signals are harder to embed without distorting meaning.

import re
import random
import numpy as np
from transformers import AutoTokenizer

SYNONYMS = {
    'large': ['big', 'substantial', 'considerable', 'significant'],
    'use':   ['employ', 'utilize', 'apply', 'leverage'],
    'show':  ['demonstrate', 'reveal', 'indicate', 'display'],
    'make':  ['create', 'produce', 'generate', 'construct'],
    'high':  ['elevated', 'increased', 'greater', 'substantial'],
    'fast':  ['quick', 'rapid', 'swift', 'speedy'],
    'find':  ['discover', 'locate', 'identify', 'detect'],
}

def synonym_substitute(text, rate=0.15, seed=42):
    '''Replace ~rate fraction of words with synonyms if available.'''
    rng, words = random.Random(seed), text.split()
    return ' '.join(
        rng.choice(SYNONYMS[w.lower().strip('.,;:!?')])
        if w.lower().strip('.,;:!?') in SYNONYMS and rng.random() < rate else w
        for w in words
    )

def robustness_sweep(watermarked_text, tokenizer, vocab_size,
                     edit_rates=None, n_trials=20):
    '''Measure detection rate under increasing synonym substitution rates.'''
    from detect_watermark import detect_watermark  # defined in Statistical Detection section
    if edit_rates is None:
        edit_rates = [0.0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.50]
    print(f'{"Edit rate":>10} | {"Detect rate":>12} | {"Mean z":>8}')
    print('-' * 36)
    for rate in edit_rates:
        hits, zs = [], []
        for trial in range(n_trials):
            edited = synonym_substitute(watermarked_text, rate=rate, seed=trial)
            res = detect_watermark(edited, tokenizer, vocab_size)
            hits.append(int(res['detected'])); zs.append(res['z'])
        print(f'{rate:>10.0%} | {np.mean(hits):>12.2f} | {np.mean(zs):>8.2f}')

Distortion-Free Watermarking

Distortion-free watermarks (Christ et al., 2023; Kuditipudi et al., 2023) embed detectable signals without changing the sampling distribution — the marginal distribution over tokens is identical to standard (non-watermarked) sampling. This is achieved using inverse transform sampling with seeded randomness: to sample from a distribution P, one generates uniform random numbers u ~ Uniform(0,1) and applies the inverse CDF. If u is replaced by a pseudorandom sequence derived from the context (a hash of preceding tokens), the resulting token sequence follows exactly the same marginal distribution as standard sampling, but carries a detectable pattern in the relationship between the random seeds and the selected tokens. The key mathematical insight is that the joint distribution over (tokens, seeds) reveals the watermark, even though the marginal distribution over tokens alone is unchanged.

The EXP (exponential) sampling watermark is the most studied distortion-free scheme. At each step, it generates Gumbel random variables g_i = -log(-log(u_i)) for each vocabulary token i, where u_i is drawn from a pseudorandom generator seeded by the context hash. The next token is selected as argmax(log P_i + g_i), which is equivalent to sampling from P in distribution (Gumbel-max trick) but carries a detectable pattern. Detection computes a score based on the expected vs. observed relationship between the pseudorandom seeds and the chosen tokens: if the watermark is present, the chosen token consistently corresponds to the highest Gumbel draw, which is detectable in aggregate. Distortion-free watermarks achieve near-identical text quality to unmodified sampling, making them preferable when quality is paramount, but they are generally less robust to editing than the green-red list scheme at equal detection thresholds.

import torch
import hashlib
import numpy as np
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

def gumbel_keys(vocab_size, prev_token, step, secret_key='wm-key-2024'):
    '''Generate seeded Gumbel noise for each vocabulary token (EXP sampling).'''
    seed_str = f'{secret_key}:{prev_token}:{step}'
    seed = int(hashlib.sha256(seed_str.encode()).hexdigest(), 16) % (2**32)
    rng  = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    u    = rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, vocab_size).clip(1e-10, 1 - 1e-10)
    return torch.tensor(-np.log(-np.log(u)), dtype=torch.float32)

def exp_watermark_generate(model, tokenizer, prompt, max_new=200,
                            secret_key='wm-key-2024', device='cuda'):
    '''EXP (Gumbel-max) distortion-free watermark: same marginal dist as argmax.'''
    model.eval()
    enc   = tokenizer(prompt, return_tensors='pt').to(device)
    ids   = enc['input_ids']
    vocab = model.config.vocab_size
    for step in range(max_new):
        with torch.no_grad():
            logits = model(ids).logits[0, -1, :]
        log_probs = torch.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)
        prev  = ids[0, -1].item()
        gkeys = gumbel_keys(vocab, prev, step, secret_key).to(device)
        # Gumbel-max trick: argmax(log_prob + Gumbel) ~ categorical(softmax)
        nxt   = (log_probs + gkeys).argmax(keepdim=True).unsqueeze(0)
        ids   = torch.cat([ids, nxt], dim=1)
        if nxt.item() == tokenizer.eos_token_id:
            break
    return tokenizer.decode(ids[0], skip_special_tokens=True)

def compare_ppl(model, tokenizer, std_text, wm_text, device='cuda'):
    '''Compare perplexity of standard vs EXP-watermarked text.'''
    model.eval()
    def ppl(t):
        enc = tokenizer(t, return_tensors='pt').to(device)
        with torch.no_grad():
            loss = model(**enc, labels=enc['input_ids']).loss
        return float(torch.exp(loss))
    print(f'Standard PPL: {ppl(std_text):.2f} | EXP watermark PPL: {ppl(wm_text):.2f}')

The distortion-free property comes with a practical detection challenge: the z-score test used for green-red watermarks does not directly apply. Detection instead requires the secret key to reconstruct the Gumbel seeds at each position, then compute a likelihood ratio between the hypothesis that the tokens were selected by Gumbel-max (watermarked) versus that they were selected by any other process. This requires O(n × vocab_size) computation per sequence for a brute-force detector, though optimized algorithms reduce this to O(n) using properties of the Gumbel distribution. Because the marginal token distribution is unchanged, distortion-free watermarks are completely undetectable to an adversary without the secret key — they cannot be distinguished from non-watermarked text by any statistical test that does not have access to the key. This is a strong security property not shared by the green-red list scheme, where a determined adversary who knows the hash function can detect and remove the watermark.

All Current Watermarks Are Breakable: All current watermarks are breakable by a determined adversary who knows the watermarking algorithm — they function as deterrents and detection tools for good-faith use, not as cryptographically strong guarantees. A paraphrase attack using a second LLM can remove most watermarks by regenerating the text semantically. The distortion-free EXP scheme is secure against adversaries without the key but not against those who can run their own LLM to regenerate the content. Watermarks are best understood as raising the cost of evasion, not preventing it entirely.

Key Takeaways

  • Green-red list watermarking (Kirchenbauer et al.) biases sampling toward a hash-seeded green token subset, detectable via z-score with near-zero FPR on human text at z_threshold=4.
  • Detection requires only the tokenizer, vocabulary size, and hash key — not model weights — making it a lightweight, scalable screening tool deployable independently of the model.
  • Text quality impact of the green-red watermark is low at δ=2.0 (sub-1 PPL increase) but grows with δ; δ=2–3 is the practical sweet spot for reliability without perceptible quality loss.
  • EXP (Gumbel-max) sampling is distortion-free — the marginal token distribution is identical to standard sampling — but requires a secret key for detection and is less robust to editing.
  • Semantic watermarks group synonyms into the same partition using embedding-space similarity, improving robustness to synonym substitution by 15–25 percentage points over token-list schemes.
  • Paraphrase attacks (regenerating content with another LLM) break most watermarks by resetting token-level statistics; no current scheme is robustly secure against a paraphrase adversary.
  • Robustness and detection power trade off against each other: schemes that survive heavy editing embed weaker signals per token and require longer sequences for reliable detection.
  • Watermarks serve as deterrents and attribution tools for good-faith use, not cryptographic guarantees — the appropriate framing is raising the cost of evasion, not preventing it.
